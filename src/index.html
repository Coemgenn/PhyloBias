The history you test is the history you get

A pathogen’s phylogenetic tree is built from the samples that happened to get sequenced, which makes it prone to ascertainment and sampling bias. Some regions test early and broadly. Others test late, or only the sickest patients. Cases that were never sampled leave no trace. The result is a tree that can look complete and confident while telling an inaccurate story of how the pathogen evolved and spread. This tool lets you change those testing parameters and watch the reconstructed history diverge from a known truth.

Testing policy

Omniscient mode is not a real possibility — it is a control experiment. Every infection is sequenced the moment it happens, asymptomatic cases included, so you can see how the tree would be reconstructed under perfect testing. It is still not identical to the known truth, because even a complete set of genomes only records mutations, not movements: two regions holding the same genotype are indistinguishable, parsimony has to break the ties that leaves, and dates come from a clock regression rather than a calendar. Those residual errors are the method’s floor — everything worse than this, elsewhere on the page, came from sampling.

Method & sources

  • Substitution rate. The Mutation rate slider sets the molecular clock, scaled to generate branch lengths consistent with respiratory-virus evolution. RNA viruses typically evolve at 1–5 × 10−3 substitutions per site per year, depending on family and protein region.
  • Transmission chain. Serial interval (time between successive infections) is modelled as Gamma(2.5, 2.6 days; mean 6.5 days). Secondary cases follow a negative-binomial distribution with dispersion k=0.5, capturing superspreading common in respiratory outbreaks.
  • Root-to-tip regression. The phylogenetic root is inferred by regressing sequence divergence against sampling date, following TempEst (Rambaut et al. 2016). The x-intercept estimates the most recent common ancestor.
  • Ancestral-state reconstruction. Ancestral regions are inferred by Fitch parsimony, which selects the region most consistent with each mutation's tips. This method is biased toward the most-sampled region (Lemey et al. 2010), even when another region held the true ancestor. Bayesian methods (BEAST, MrBayes) mitigate this bias through joint modelling of sampling effort.
  • Variant nomenclature. The WHO adopted Greek-letter naming (Alpha, Beta, Gamma, …) in May 2021 for epidemiologically significant variants of concern, standardising naming across viruses and avoiding geographic stigma (WHO 2021).
  • Test positivity. High positivity (≥5%) signals overwhelmed testing capacity and undetected cases. During SARS-CoV-2, peak per-capita testing reached 5–10% of population per week in developed countries, setting a reference for adequate surveillance breadth.
  • Testing channels. Community screening swabs random people (detection probability = prevalence; unbiased but wasteful). Hospital intake swabs admissions at ~100% detection but captures only severe/critical cases and saturates once capacity exceeds ward census.
  • Model caveat. This outbreak reaches ~10,000 people with peak prevalence far higher than reality, making random screening more efficient here than in practice. The advantage of hospital sampling is therefore understated.
  • Method caveat. The bias shown here is emergent, not hardcoded—it arises from running textbook Fitch parsimony on an unevenly sampled tip set. Production methods reduce but cannot fully eliminate this failure mode.
